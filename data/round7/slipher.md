## Completeness summary

The instrument is a **prism spectrograph at the f/16 focus of a refractor, with a fast photographic lens as camera**. Sourced: Clark objective 61 cm clear aperture, 983 cm focal length (f/16; S04); Brashear collimator doublet 489.1 mm focal length, 30.5 mm aperture, f/16 (S04); the nebular dispersing element is a "very dense" 64 deg prism, one prism for most nebulae and two for two of the 25 (S13, S17), 64.5 deg for the 1917 NGC 1068 plate (S17B); camera: a Voigtlander lens of speed about 1:2.5 and "3 1/4-inch" focal length (S15, S17), which with the collimator gives the "six times" focal-length ratio of S16; linear dispersion about 140 A/mm "in the violet" and an "equivalent slit-width" of about 0.06 mm (S17); Seed 30 plates, iron-vanadium spark (S15, S17); exposures from 6 h 50 min (M31, 1912) to 20-40 h (S13, S17); the M31 velocities from four plates (-284, -296, -308, -301; mean -300 km/s) and the 25-nebula table (S13, S17); the stellar three-prism train (Schott O 102 prisms of 62 deg 52', measured indices, dispersions at Hgamma, two cameras of 386 and 471 mm) is documented in complete detail (S04, S05).

Gaps that matter: (1) the **64 deg prism's glass, size and minimum-deviation setting are not published** (NOT FOUND). Slipher's own 140 A/mm with a dense flint of the type of his 1904 single prism fixes the glass to a dispersion-equivalent: proposed Schott N-SF15 (INFERRED; reproduces 141 A/mm at 4415 A and 131 A/mm at 4340 A). (2) The camera's focal length is given only as "3 1/4-inch" (a 1915 popular summary); reading it as 82.55 mm is consistent with two other statements (collimator "six times" the camera, speed gain "fully 30-fold"). The lens model and the plate size/tilt are NOT FOUND. (3) The "equivalent slit-width" of 0.06 mm is read as the slit image on the plate (as Thompson does); the actual slit opening is not stated (the reading gives 0.355 mm = 7.5 arcsec; Thompson's 8.25 arcsec cannot be reproduced). (4) No nebular line width, no resolving limit and no velocity uncertainty smaller than "as much as 100 kilometers" is published. (5) The layout figure for the nebular camera is a single photograph without scale (T13, Fig. 1); Hearnshaw 2009 (book) was not accessible and would settle the glass, the camera model and the stellar-era mount.

**Recommended preset**: "Lowell 24-inch refractor + Brashear spectrograph, nebular configuration (1912-1917)": D = 0.61 m, refractor focal ratio 16.1, slit 7.5 arcsec (0.06 mm equivalent), lens collimator 489.1 mm / 30.5 mm beam, one 64 deg prism (N-SF15) at minimum deviation near 4400 A, camera lens 82.55 mm f/2.5, photographic plate. Setting menu: (A) one 64 deg prism, 140 A/mm (default; M31, 1912-1917); (B) two 64.5 deg prisms (NGC 1068, 1917; about half the scale, 70 A/mm DERIVED); (C) the original stellar three-prism train (O 102 = SF2 prisms of 62.88 deg, 471.4 mm long camera, 11.4 A/mm at Hgamma, 0.02 mm slit), a good contrast showing why the nebular camera trades dispersion for speed.

## Lowell 24-inch Clark refractor / Brashear spectrograph, nebular configuration (Slipher, 1912-1917)

Era and layout: refractor objective -> (optional 95 mm correcting doublet, not used for the nebular plates) -> slit plate at the objective focus (slit jaws of speculum metal, tongue and spectrum-width jaws; Huggins guiding by the light the jaws stop) -> collimator doublet (489.1 mm, 30.5 mm) in line with the tube -> one 64 deg dense flint prism (or two) near minimum deviation -> Voigtlander camera lens (82.55 mm, f/2.5) standing about 69 deg from the collimator axis (DERIVED) -> plate (Seed 30). The whole spectrograph rotates about the optical axis; the stellar three-prism train uses the same collimator and a camera nearly alongside it (total deviation 176.5 deg). All elements are lenses; **no on-axis/off-axis question arises** (no mirror, no obscuration).

### Glass section (the 64 deg prism needs a dispersion formula)

No Sellmeier formula exists for the historic Jena glass O 102 or for the unnamed 64 deg glass. Both are modeled by modern Schott catalog glasses chosen by fitting the measured indices of S04 (script `glass_scan.py`, `fit2.py` in the scratchpad; fits after removing a constant index offset).

**O 102 (three-prism train): SF2 (INFERRED).** The eight measured indices of the average prism (S04, Table I; Hα 1.64344 ... K 1.68826) are matched by Schott SF2 with an offset of -0.0015 and rms residual 7e-5 over Hα-K (next best N-SF2, 1.6e-4). SF2 at Hα, D2, Hβ, 4529, Hγ, Hδ, H, K = 1.6421, 1.64755, 1.66123, 1.66816, 1.67298, 1.68037, 1.68529, 1.68668 (DERIVED from the SF2 Sellmeier constants below). The model then gives 11.9 A/mm at Hγ for three 62.88 deg prisms with the 471.4 mm camera (measured 11.4, 4.7% high) and 14.6 vs 14.4 A/mm with the 385.7 mm camera.

**64 deg nebular prism: N-SF15 (INFERRED, dispersion-equivalent).** The glass is not published. The only measured dense-flint indices are for the 1904 single prism (n = 1.6905, 1.6954, 1.7131, 1.7310 at Hα, D2, Hβ, Hγ); N-SF15 reproduces them with an offset of +0.0016 and 0.0016 rms (nd 1.69892, vd 30.20). Required dispersion: 140 A/mm with an 82.55 mm camera is 17.85 arcsec/A. For a 64.0 deg prism at minimum deviation N-SF15 gives 131 A/mm at 4340 A, 141 A/mm at 4415 A and 212 A/mm at Hβ, with a minimum deviation of 68.9 deg at 4340 A (DERIVED; the prism-to-camera angle). N-SF8 (nd 1.68894, vd 31.31) gives 144 A/mm at 4340 A and is the alternative if the explorer should match 140 A/mm exactly at Hγ. Slipher's "140 A/mm in the violet" has no stated wavelength, so either glass is within the uncertainty; flag the choice in the preset.

Formula: Schott Sellmeier, n^2 - 1 = sum B_i lambda^2/(lambda^2 - C_i), lambda in um (same form as the round-1 SCHOTT glasses).

**How the plate enters the explorer (no pixels).** The photographic plate is not sampled; the line shape is set by the projected slit image (0.06 mm) convolved with the lens image and the emulsion grain. Published: the equivalent slit width 0.06 mm, and (stellar era, three-prism train, S04 pp. 14-15) a plate resolving limit equal to about 0.022 mm (0.25 A at 11.4 A/mm, DERIVED) on the most rapid plates. No nebular line width is published, no grain size. Recommendation: model the detector as a continuous plate, 0.06 mm projected slit as the resolution element (FWHM 8.4 A at 140 A/mm), and, if the model needs a pixel, a 0.02 mm effective sampling (EFFECTIVE, from the 0.022 mm plate limit, 3 samples per slit image); plate format about 2 x 3 inch (S04 p. 5, stellar holders; the nebular holder is NOT FOUND), spectrum 6.7 x 1.9 mm.

### Conflicts between sources

- Camera aperture of the long camera: 36 mm (S04 p. 11) vs 35 mm (S05 p. 318). The explorer need not use either.
- Stock long-camera focal ratio: Slipher gives 1:13 (471.4 mm, 36 mm) and a 1:16 collimator (S04); Thompson 2011 and Smith 2013 give f/14.2, Thompson 2013 "f/14". Not resolvable from Slipher; the nebular camera is unaffected.
- Slit on the sky: this record 7.46 arcsec (0.06 mm x 489.1/82.55 / 47.66 um per arcsec); Thompson 2013 (p. 5) 8.25 arcsec, factor unstated (would need a collimator-to-camera ratio of 6.55, i.e. a 74.7 mm camera). Both rest on reading "equivalent slit-width" as the plate width.
- S04 Table IV: the top row of the single flint prism (printed Hδ, deviation 60 deg 23.8', n = 1.7360) is out of line with the dispersion (n(Hγ) - n(Hβ) = 0.018 but n(Hδ) - n(Hγ) = 0.005; the crown prism's top row has the same anomaly); not used in the fit.
- S04 Table II angular dispersion 36.8 (unit mark read as arcsec per A) vs 206265/(471 x 11.4) = 38.4 for the long camera; the short camera agrees (36.85). Not used.
- Number of nebulae observed: 25 (S17 p. 404) vs "about thirty" (S17O p. 305, the same year).
- Single prism timing: Smith 2013 (p. 155) says a single prism was adopted by November 1910; Thompson 2013 (p. 4) says the 1911 attempt used a prism "with too low a dispersion" and the dense prism came in 1912 (S13: "last summer"). The 64 deg prism is therefore a 1912 addition.
- Where a camera called "short camera" was used for the early M31 plates (S13: "using the short camera" a few years before) is the 385.7 mm lens, not the Voigtlander.

### Notes

- **What the model needs that the explorer lacks**: a prism as the MAIN disperser (a single 64 deg prism near minimum deviation, optionally two in series), a photographic detector without pixels (continuous plate; slit-image resolution element), a lens collimator and a lens camera of focal ratio about 2.7 (camera f/2.5 behind a 30.5 mm beam), a refractor feed (f/16, no central obscuration), and a long exposure time in hours (20-40 h, over several nights).
- **Speed for extended sources**: the camera focal ratio alone sets the surface-brightness speed (S13, S17; T13 eq. 1: speed proportional to w'P/F^2). With the 30.5 mm collimated beam in an 82.55 mm lens the working ratio is 2.7, so the lens aperture (33 mm) just accepts the beam.
- **Dispersion is not constant**: for the prism the dispersion falls from about 212 A/mm at Hβ to 131 A/mm at Hγ and below (N-SF15), a factor 1.6 across F-H, which is what the 1913 remark about the shift at the violet end being "twice" that at the blue end reflects (a strong non-linear scale). The explorer's prism code gives this directly.
- Stellar-era configuration (C) differs in everything: 3 prisms, a 0.02 mm slit, 471 or 386 mm cameras on 2 x 3 inch plates; exposures of minutes.
- The velocity-measure method (Hartmann spectrocomparator against a standard plate) is not an explorer feature; only the shift in mm or pixel-equivalents is, and the preset can quote 0.031 mm for 300 km/s at 4340 A.
- Spelling: American; quotes verbatim (including the OCR-transcription of old hyphenation).
